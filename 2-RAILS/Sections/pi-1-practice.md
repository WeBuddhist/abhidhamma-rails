## Mātikā

[[#^toc-1|↑↑↑]]

The Mātikā is the founding matrix of the entire Dhammasaṅgaṇī — a comprehensive classification of all phenomena (dhammā) into 22 triads (tikā) and 142 pairs (dukā), together constituting the shared index of all seven Abhidhamma books. The commentary explains that it is the Buddha's own word (*jinavacanabhūtā sabbaññubuddhena desitā*), providing a complete map of experience organized by ethical quality, resultance, mental relationship, and liberation-potential.

**Doing Less Harm.** Recognize that every mental event belongs to one of three moral categories — wholesome (*kusala*), unwholesome (*akusala*), or indeterminate (*abyākata*). Confusion about which category a given action or impulse falls into is itself a form of harm. Study the matrix so that this confusion is reduced. When unsure whether an act of body, speech, or mind is wholesome or not, pause and consult the criteria: is it blameless (*anavajja*)? Does it produce pleasant results (*sukhavipāka*)? If not, restrain.

**Doing More Good.** The Mātikā reveals the full scope of what is worth cultivating: not only the ethical basics (kusala kammapathā) but refined meditative states, the path factors, and ultimately the supramundane (*lokuttara*) dhammas. Use the matrix as a checklist of possibilities — wherever a wholesome state is present in the classification, there is an opportunity to develop it further.

**Cultivating the Mind.** Contemplate the triads and pairs as a living map of your moment-to-moment experience. In meditation, when a state arises, ask: Is this kusala or akusala? Is it accompanied by a root (*sahetuka*) or rootless (*ahetuka*)? Is it conditioned (*sappaccaya*) or has it touched the unconditioned (*asaṅkhata*)? This inquiry — practiced with lightness rather than rigidity — is itself a form of *dhammānupassanā* (contemplation of dhammas).

### Tikamātikā

[[#^toc-1-1|↑↑↑]]

The Tikamātikā comprises 22 triads, forming a single group (*eko paricchedo*) within the matrix. Each triad classifies phenomena along three terms, with the first triad — *kusala / akusala / abyākata* — as the root around which all others revolve. The commentary defines *kusala* as bearing the marks of blamelessness and pleasant results (*anavajjasukhavipākalakkhaṇā*), *akusala* as blameworthy and painful-resultant (*sāvajjadukkhavipākalakkhaṇā*), and *abyākata* as neither-resultant (*avipākalakkhaṇā*). The remaining triads map feelings, resultance, grasping, defilement, applied/sustained thought, rapture, abandonment by path, accumulation vs. cessation, training, scope, objects, quality, destination, temporal status, location, and materiality.

**Doing Less Harm.** Of the 22 triads, the most urgent for daily conduct are the first (*kusalattikaṃ*), the fifth (*saṃkiliṭṭhasaṃkilesikattiko* — the triad of defilement), and the eighth (*dassanattiko* — abandonment by seeing or development). The fifth triad reveals that phenomena can be both defiled and defiling: when a state is *saṃkiliṭṭhasaṃkilesikā* (defiled and capable of defiling others), it corrupts the continuum and serves as a fuel for further defilement. To do less harm, recognise when you are in such a state — anger that inflames, craving that spreads — and disengage before it contaminates speech or action.

**Doing More Good.** The tenth triad (*ācayagāmittiko*) is particularly important for orienting practice. Conditioned kusala and akusala states lead to *ācaya* — accumulation in the round (*sāsavakusalākusalā*). Only the noble path (*ariyamagga*) leads to *apacaya* — cessation of accumulation. This triad motivates the practitioner to go beyond merely good actions in the world and actively cultivate the path. Use the eleventh triad (*sekkhattiko*) similarly: a trainee (*sekkhā*) is one still learning; the path is not yet complete. Take up the posture of a learner rather than one who has arrived.

**Cultivating the Mind.** The second triad (*vedanāttiko*) underpins all mindfulness-of-feeling (*vedanānupassanā*): every experience has a hedonic tone — pleasant (*sukhā*), painful (*dukkhā*), or neutral (*adukkhamasukhā*). In sitting meditation, simply note the feeling-tone of each moment without grasping or pushing away. The seventh triad (*pītatiko*) maps the meditative progression from rapture (*pīti*) through happiness (*sukha*) to equanimity (*upekkhā*) — a template for jhāna development. The fifteenth triad (*micchattattiko*) warns that some beings are fixed on the wrong path with certain results; others are fixed on the right; many are unfixed (*aniyatā*). The unfixed state is an opportunity: one has not yet fallen irreversibly into wrong determination. Use it now.

### Dukamātikā

[[#^toc-1-2|↑↑↑]]

The Dukamātikā comprises 100 pairs of the Abhidhamma matrix plus 42 suttanta pairs — 142 in total, grouped into 14 sections (*catuddasa paricchedā*). The pairs are organized into seven *gocchakā* (clusters of related pairs), the *cūḷantaradukā* (unrelated intermediate pairs), the *mahantaradukā* (great intermediate pairs), and supplementary groups. Together, these 142 pairs map every aspect of conditioned and unconditioned existence from the perspective of roots, defilement-categories, mental structure, liberation-orientation, and practical skill. The commentary explains that the Buddha structured these clusters so that pairs related to each other by content and logical connection (*aññamaññasambandha*) are grouped as *gocchakā* — held together like clusters of fruit on a stalk.

**Doing Less Harm.** The Dukamātikā as a whole trains the practitioner to distinguish what defiles and what purifies across every domain of experience. The seven defilement-related gocchakā (āsava, saṃyojana, gantha, ogha, yoga, nīvaraṇa, parāmāsa) enumerate the forces that drag beings downward. Regularly reviewing these clusters — even as bare lists — cultivates the recognition: "This is a fetter. This is a flood. This is a hindrance." Recognition precedes restraint.

**Doing More Good.** The suttantika pairs at the end of the Dukamātikā provide the most accessible practical guidance. They range from ethical pairs (hirī/ottappa — shame and moral dread, the guardians of the world), to friendship (kalyāṇamittatā), to the full meditation path (samatho/vipassanā), to liberation itself (vijjā/vimutti). These pairs name the goods worth cultivating in sequence, from the foundations of virtue up through insight to liberation.

**Cultivating the Mind.** Study the Dukamātikā as a paired taxonomy: each pair reveals what a thing is by showing what it is not. This dialectical structure trains the discerning mind (*paṭisambhidā*). In meditation, practice holding both terms of a pair simultaneously — "this state is conditioned (*sappaccayā*); Nibbāna alone is unconditioned (*appaccayā*)" — as a way of orienting consciousness toward the unconditioned.

#### Hetugocchakaṃ

[[#^toc-1-2-1|↑↑↑]]

The Hetugocchaka consists of six pairs organized around the concept of *hetu* — root or foundation (*mūlaṭṭha*). The three unwholesome roots are *lobha* (greed), *dosa* (hatred), and *moha* (delusion); the three wholesome roots are *alobha* (non-greed), *adosa* (non-hatred), and *amoha* (non-delusion). A state that *is* a root (*hetū dhammā*) conditions and underlies a broad range of associated phenomena; a state that merely *has* a root (*sahetukā*) is supported by and co-arises with such a root. The commentary notes that the final pair of this cluster (*na hetū kho pana dhammā sahetukāpi ahetukāpi*) uses the particle *kho pana* to indicate an additional distinction — there are also states that are neither roots themselves nor supported by roots.

**Doing Less Harm.** The unwholesome roots are the ultimate sources of harmful action. Any act of body, speech, or mind that is driven by *lobha*, *dosa*, or *moha* is *akusala*. To do less harm, learn to identify which root is operative in a difficult moment: is this craving (*lobha*) reaching for something? Is this aversion (*dosa*) pushing something away? Is this confusion (*moha*) failing to see clearly? Naming the root weakens its hold.

**Doing More Good.** The wholesome roots — *alobha* (generosity, letting go), *adosa* (goodwill, kindness), and *amoha* (wisdom, clarity) — are the foundations of all meritorious action. Every act of giving flows from *alobha*; every act of loving-kindness from *adosa*; every moment of clear understanding from *amoha*. Deliberately cultivating generosity, goodwill, and discernment is directly cultivating the wholesome roots.

**Cultivating the Mind.** In formal meditation, use the roots as an internal diagnostic tool. Before settling into the breath or a meditation object, briefly check: "What is the root of the mind right now? Is it supported by wholesome or unwholesome roots?" A mind rooted in *alobha, adosa, amoha* is already well prepared for *samādhi*. If an unwholesome root is present, acknowledge it, apply the antidote (e.g., reflect on impermanence to counter lobha; extend goodwill to dissolve dosa), and re-establish the wholesome base before proceeding.

#### Cūḷantaradukaṃ

[[#^toc-1-2-2|↑↑↑]]

The Cūḷantaraduka consists of seven pairs that are placed together not because of mutual logical connection (unlike the gocchakā) but simply by their shared nature as pairs, interleaved between the major gocchakā — hence *cūḷa* (small/lesser) *antara* (between). The seven pairs are: conditioned/unconditioned (*sappaccayā/appaccayā*), compounded/uncompounded (*saṅkhatā/asaṅkhatā*), visible/invisible (*sanidassanā/anidassanā*), resistant/non-resistant (*sappaṭighā/appaṭighā*), material/immaterial (*rūpino/arūpino*), mundane/supramundane (*lokiyā/lokuttarā*), knowable-by-some-consciousness/not-knowable (*kenaci viññeyyā/kenaci na viññeyyā*).

**Doing Less Harm.** The pair *lokiyā/lokuttarā* is a compass for practice. Every mundane (*lokiyā*) attainment — wealth, health, reputation, even meditative calm — remains within the round of existence (*vaṭṭa*) and is therefore liable to change and loss. Clinging to mundane attainments as ultimate causes harm — both to oneself (through disappointment) and to others (through competitive grasping). Recognizing that all conditioned phenomena are *sappaccayā* (arising dependent on conditions) and therefore impermanent is the antidote.

**Doing More Good.** The pair *saṅkhatā/asaṅkhatā* reminds the practitioner that all conditioned phenomena arise, persist briefly, and cease — only Nibbāna (*asaṅkhata*) is beyond this process. Direct effort toward what culminates in the unconditioned: generosity that weakens attachment, virtue that purifies intention, meditation that sees through conditionality.

**Cultivating the Mind.** In insight meditation, the pair *sappaccayā/appaccayā* — conditioned vs. unconditioned — can be used as a direct contemplative anchor. As each mental or physical phenomenon arises and passes, note its conditioned nature: "This arose because of conditions; it will cease when those conditions cease." Sustained practice with this observation opens the mind to the recognition of the unconditioned as the natural stopping-point of craving.

#### Āsavagocchakaṃ

[[#^toc-1-2-3|↑↑↑]]

The Āsavagocchaka consists of six pairs built around the concept of *āsava* — the deep taints or fermentations. The commentary explains the term *āsava* through three images: like old fermented liquor (*cirapārivāsiyaṭṭhena madirādayo viya*), they have been flowing through existence for an immeasurably long time; they flow (*savanti*) from the eye-door all the way to the summit of existence (*bhavaggā*), permeating all experience; and they flow forth (*āyataṃ savanti*) as the long stream of saṃsāric suffering, which cannot arise without them. The four āsavā are *kāmāsava* (taint of sensuality), *bhavāsava* (taint of existence), *diṭṭhāsava* (taint of views), and *avijjāsava* (taint of ignorance). States that *are* āsavā are the taints themselves; states that are *sāsavā* (tainted, āsava-associated) include all phenomena that can serve as objects for the āsavā to flow through.

**Doing Less Harm.** The āsavā do not arise suddenly — they flow through the habitual channels of sense contact and unexamined experience. Every unguarded sense impression (*indriyesu aguttadvāratā*) provides the āsavā with fresh material. To do less harm, close these channels through sense restraint: at the moment of seeing, hearing, smelling, tasting, touching, and thinking, avoid grasping at the pleasant aspects or pushing away the unpleasant. This restraint directly starves the āsavā of fuel.

**Doing More Good.** The *anāsavā* dhammas — states untainted by the āsavā — include the noble path factors and Nibbāna itself. To cultivate the anāsavā, strengthen the path: develop right view to counter *diṭṭhāsava*; use the contemplation of impermanence to erode *bhavāsava*; train in sense restraint and renunciation for *kāmāsava*; and cultivate wisdom directly to uproot *avijjāsava*.

**Cultivating the Mind.** The āsavā operate at a very deep level — they color even the most refined meditative states in the three lower planes. In meditation, watch for subtle tones of satisfaction with the meditation itself: "I am a good meditator," "This jhāna is pleasurable" — these can be expressions of *bhavāsava*. The goal of practice is to develop the insight that fully uproots all four taints, making one a *khīṇāsava* — one in whom the fermentations are exhausted.

#### Saṃyojanagocchakaṃ

[[#^toc-1-2-4|↑↑↑]]

The Saṃyojanagocchaka consists of six pairs centered on *saṃyojana* — fetters. The commentary defines them as states that *bind beings in the round* (*taṃ puggalaṃ vaṭṭasmiṃ saṃyojenti bandhantīti saṃyojanā*). The ten fetters are: *sakkāyadiṭṭhi* (personality view), *vicikicchā* (doubt), *sīlabbataparāmāsa* (clinging to rites and rituals), *kāmarāga* (sensual desire), *paṭigha* (ill-will), *rūparāga* (attachment to form-sphere existence), *arūparāga* (attachment to formless-sphere existence), *māna* (conceit), *uddhacca* (restlessness), and *avijjā* (ignorance). *Saṃyojaniyā* dhammas are those that can serve as the object for fetters to bind to — the ārammaṇa-condition for the fetters' operation.

**Doing Less Harm.** The lower three fetters (*sakkāyadiṭṭhi, vicikicchā, sīlabbataparāmāsa*) are particularly relevant to daily life. Sakkāyadiṭṭhi — the view "this is my self, this is mine" — drives the most persistent forms of harm: defensive reaction, possessiveness, conflict. Examine where this view is operating. Vicikicchā — debilitating doubt — paralyses wholesome action; cultivate confidence through direct investigation of the Dhamma rather than through blind faith. Sīlabbataparāmāsa — clinging to mere form of practice as if it were the substance — leads to performing virtuous actions without their purpose.

**Doing More Good.** The progress of practice corresponds directly to the successive abandonment of fetters. Stream-entry abandons the first three; once-returning weakens kāmarāga and paṭigha; non-returning eliminates them; arahantship removes all ten. This structure maps out the path in practical terms: at every stage, there is identifiable work to do. Use the fetter-list not as a source of discouragement but as a precise diagnostic: "Which fetters are still present? What does the practice require right now?"

**Cultivating the Mind.** In meditation, the fetters manifest as impediments to deepening. Sakkāyadiṭṋhi surfaces as identification with the meditating "self." Kāmarāga surfaces as distraction toward pleasant sense objects. Uddhacca surfaces as mental restlessness. Rather than suppressing these as nuisances, use them as objects of insight: see each fetter as an impermanent, conditioned arising in the mind-stream — not "my" fetter, but *a* fetter arising in dependence on conditions.

#### Ganthagocchakaṃ

[[#^toc-1-2-5|↑↑↑]]

The Ganthagocchaka consists of six pairs built around *gantha* — bodily knots. The commentary defines them as states that *bind and tie beings in the round from rebirth to death* (*cutipaṭisandhivasena vaṭṭasmiṃ ganthenti ghaṭentīti ganthā*). The four bodily knots (*kāyaganthā*) are: *abhijjhā kāyagantha* (covetousness), *byāpāda* (ill-will), *sīlabbataparāmāsa* (clinging to rites and rituals), and *idaṃsaccābhinivesa* (insistence that one's own view alone is true). *Ganthaniyā* dhammas are those that can serve as objects to which the knots bind — the ārammaṇa-condition for the ganthā.

**Doing Less Harm.** The four knots are particularly relevant to interpersonal and community harm. Covetousness (*abhijjhā*) binds through desire for what belongs to others. Ill-will (*byāpāda*) knots through resentment and the wish to harm. Ritual-clinging (*sīlabbataparāmāsa*) knots through insisting that one's own form of religious practice is the only valid one. Dogmatic-truth-insistence (*idaṃsaccābhinivesa*) — the knot of "this alone is true, all else is false" — is the root of sectarian harm and closed-minded dispute. To do less harm in community and in discourse, specifically work against this fourth knot: practice holding views with some tentativeness.

**Doing More Good.** The antidote to the knots is developed through the cultivation of their opposites: *anabhijjhā* (non-covetousness / generosity), *abyāpāda* (non-ill-will / goodwill), *sammādiṭṭhi* (right view, which loosens both sīlabbataparāmāsa and idaṃsaccābhinivesa). Every act of generosity loosens the covetousness-knot; every practice of goodwill meditation loosens the ill-will-knot.

**Cultivating the Mind.** In sitting meditation, the ganthā surface as subtle pulls. Watch for the mind reaching for an object it finds pleasant (abhijjhā), or stiffening against something unpleasant (byāpāda). Notice when there is a subtle insistence that the meditation *should* go a particular way (sīlabbataparāmāsa in a meditation context). See these as knots in the fabric of experience — not enemies to be fought, but patterns to be understood and released.

#### Oghagocchakaṃ

[[#^toc-1-2-6|↑↑↑]]

The Oghagocchaka consists of six pairs built around *ogha* — floods. The commentary defines the floods as states that *cause beings to sink and drown in the round itself* (*yaṃ vaṭṭasmiṃyeva ohananti osīdāpentīti oghā*). The four floods are *kāmogha* (flood of sensuality), *bhavogha* (flood of existence), *diṭṭhogha* (flood of views), and *avijjāogha* (flood of ignorance). *Oghaniyā* dhammas are those that can be crossed by the floods — i.e., those that are objects for the floods to sweep through and take over.

**Doing Less Harm.** The image of flooding is apt: once a flood is in motion it overwhelms everything in its path. Sensual desire, when unchecked, floods the mind, distorts judgment, and carries the person into actions they later regret. Harm to oneself and others escalates when any of the four floods are running strongly. The practical antidote is to build embankments before the flood — not to wait until desire or ignorance has already overwhelmed one's restraint. Establish virtue, sense-restraint, and clear comprehension as the banks before the flood season arrives.

**Doing More Good.** The image of crossing (*taranto*) a flood appears frequently in the suttas: the goal of practice is to cross the floods — to "go beyond" (*pāraṃ gacchati*) them. Each of the four floods is crossed by its specific antidote: kāmogha by renunciation and sense restraint; bhavogha by contemplating impermanence and relinquishment; diṭṭhogha by right view and the investigation of Dhamma; avijjāogha by the full development of insight culminating in path knowledge.

**Cultivating the Mind.** In meditation, especially in extended retreats where the mind is stilled, the floods can surface with unusual force. When sensual thoughts flood meditation practice, they are not a "failure" of practice — they are the very defilements that practice is designed to make visible. Observe the flood-quality of such states: the sense of being swept along, of losing one's footing. This observation, sustained with equanimity, is itself practice.

#### Yogagocchakaṃ

[[#^toc-1-2-7|↑↑↑]]

The Yogagocchaka consists of six pairs built around *yoga* — bonds or yokes. The commentary defines them as states that *yoke beings to the round* (*vaṭṭasmiṃ yojentīti yogā*). The four bonds are *kāmayoga* (bond of sensuality), *bhavayoga* (bond of existence), *diṭṭhiyoga* (bond of views), and *avijjāyoga* (bond of ignorance) — the same four content-categories as the floods and āsavā but with the distinct metaphor of being *yoked* or *bound*. *Yoganiyā* dhammas are those that can be yoked by the bonds, analogous to the oghaniyā.

**Doing Less Harm.** The metaphor of the yoke emphasizes constraint and compulsion: someone who is yoked cannot choose freely where to go. To the extent one is yoked to sensuality, one acts out of sensual compulsion; yoked to existence, one clings desperately to continued being; yoked to views, one is driven by ideology; yoked to ignorance, one acts blindly. Reducing harm means progressively loosening these yokes. Pay particular attention to the yoga of views (*diṭṭhiyoga*): much interpersonal and collective harm arises from people acting as if their views were unquestionable facts.

**Doing More Good.** The yogas overlap with the āsavā and oghā in content but each metaphor teaches something different. The āsavā teach about the depth and antiquity of defilement (long-fermented); the oghā about its overwhelming force; the yogā about its binding quality. Recognizing the *binding* nature of these defilements motivates seeking liberation — *yoga-vimutti*, freedom from the bonds. Every act of letting go, of releasing a cherished position or habitual pleasure, is a direct loosening of the yokes.

**Cultivating the Mind.** In meditation, the yogā manifest as the sense of being pulled back into habitual patterns. Even in deep stillness, a subtle pull toward sense-memory (*kāmayoga*), toward the continuation of meditative experience (*bhavayoga*), toward preference for a particular view of practice (*diṭṭhiyoga*), or toward inattention (*avijjāyoga*) can arise. Recognizing these pulls as yokes — not as personal failings but as universal conditioned patterns — allows one to sit with them without being governed by them.

#### Nīvaraṇagocchakaṃ

[[#^toc-1-2-8|↑↑↑]]

The Nīvaraṇagocchaka consists of six pairs centered on *nīvaraṇa* — hindrances. The commentary defines them as states that *obstruct and envelop the mind* (*cittaṃ nīvaranti pariyonandhantīti nīvaraṇā*). The five hindrances are *kāmacchanda* (sensual desire), *byāpāda* (ill-will), *thīnamiddha* (sloth-and-torpor), *uddhaccakukkucca* (restlessness-and-worry), and *vicikicchā* (doubt). The Mūlaṭīkā further specifies that the hindrances are the *direct opponents of jhāna-factors*: sensual desire is opposed by one-pointedness; ill-will by joy; sloth-torpor by applied thought and energy; restlessness by equanimity; doubt by sustained thought and discriminative knowledge. *Nīvaraṇiyā* dhammas are those that can serve as objects for the hindrances to obstruct — states that nourish or activate the hindrances when attended to wrongly.

**Doing Less Harm.** In daily life, the hindrances are responsible for much unskillful action. When sensual desire (*kāmacchanda*) is strong, we reach for pleasures and harm ourselves and others through excess and misappropriation. When ill-will (*byāpāda*) is active, harsh speech, harmful action, and withdrawn behavior follow. Sloth-torpor (*thīnamiddha*) produces negligence and inattention to the needs of oneself and others. Restlessness-and-worry (*uddhaccakukkucca*) produces agitation that interferes with clear judgment. Doubt (*vicikicchā*) produces paralysis and indecision about ethical action. Reducing each hindrance has direct practical consequences in everyday conduct.

**Doing More Good.** The traditional five antidotes to the hindrances are themselves positive cultivations: (1) contemplate foulness (*asubha*) for sensual desire; (2) develop loving-kindness (*mettā*) for ill-will; (3) arouse energy and direct the mind to energizing objects for sloth-torpor; (4) cultivate mental collectedness (*samādhi*) and reflect on impermanence for restlessness-and-worry; (5) investigate and study the Dhamma for doubt. These are not merely techniques for restoring meditation — they are practices with value in their own right.

**Cultivating the Mind.** The hindrances are the most directly relevant category for formal meditation practice. Before beginning a sitting, briefly scan: which hindrances are present? Then apply their antidotes and only proceed once the mind has achieved some degree of lightness and clarity. In the Mūlaṭīkā, the hindrances are described as the specific opponents of each jhāna factor: vitakka (applied thought) counters sloth-torpor, vicāra (sustained thought) counters doubt, pīti (rapture) counters ill-will, sukha (happiness) counters restlessness, ekaggatā (one-pointedness) counters sensual desire. Understanding this mapping turns jhāna practice into a direct, systematic antidote to the hindrances.

#### Parāmāsagocchakaṃ

[[#^toc-1-2-9|↑↑↑]]

The Parāmāsagocchaka consists of five pairs centered on *parāmāsa* — perverse grasping or misapprehension. The commentary defines *parāmāsā* as states that *transgress the true nature of phenomena as impermanent, etc., and touch them in a distorted way — grasping them as permanent, etc.* (*dhammānaṃ yathābhūtaṃ aniccādiākāraṃ atikkamitvā 'nicca'ntiādivasena pavattamānā parato āmasantīti parāmāsā*). In the Abhidhamma classification, there is only one parāmāsa: *diṭṭhi* (wrong view), particularly the view that misapprehends conditioned phenomena as permanent, pleasurable, or self. *Parāmaṭṭhā* dhammas are those that are grasped in this distorted way — the objects of perverse contact.

**Doing Less Harm.** Most harmful actions have a misapprehension at their root — a distorted contact with reality. The thief believes the stolen object will truly satisfy; the angry person believes their condemnation of another is accurate. Both are expressions of *parāmāsa*: touching things through a veil of distortion. To do less harm, frequently pause and ask: "Am I seeing this situation as it actually is, or through a lens of permanence, pleasure-fantasy, or self-interest?" The Dhamma's teaching on the three characteristics (*aniccā, dukkhā, anattā*) is the direct antidote to parāmāsa.

**Doing More Good.** The cultivation of right view (*sammādiṭṭhi*) directly counters parāmāsa. This includes the study of the Dhamma, reflection on impermanence and suffering, and the development of insight wisdom (*vipassanāpaññā*) that sees phenomena as they actually are. Even a preliminary intellectual understanding — "this conditioned state will change, it cannot fully satisfy, it is not a stable self" — begins to loosen the grip of perverse grasping.

**Cultivating the Mind.** In meditation, parāmāsa manifests as the tendency to take meditation experiences — pleasant sensations, blissful states, expansive feelings — as inherently satisfying or as indicators of a special attainment. This subtle grasping can be harder to see than gross hindrances. The antidote is systematic contemplation of impermanence: even the most beautiful meditative experience arises and passes. Note its arising, note its passing, and release the grasping without abandoning the experience itself.

#### Mahantaradukaṃ

[[#^toc-1-2-10|↑↑↑]]

The Mahantaraduka consists of fourteen pairs — the "great intermediate pairs" — covering the structural relationships between mind (*citta*), mental factors (*cetasikā*), and matter (*rūpa*). The commentary explains: *sārammaṇā* are states arising with an object; *anārammaṇā* lack any object (matter and Nibbāna). *Cittā* are mind — characterized by knowing (*cintanaṭṭha*) or by diversity (*vicittaṭṭha*). *Cetasikā* are mental factors — inseparably joined to consciousness (*avippayogavasena cetasi niyuttā*). *Cittasaṃsaṭṭhā* are intimately blended with consciousness from moment to moment; *cittasamuṭṭhānā* arise with consciousness as their source; *cittasahabhuno* arise simultaneously with consciousness; *cittānuparivattino* follow after consciousness through each phase. *Ajjhattikā* are phenomena in one's own continuum; *bāhirā* are external. *Upādā* are derived matter (*upādārūpa*) — arising in dependence on the four great elements.

**Doing Less Harm.** The pair *ajjhattikā/bāhirā* — internal and external phenomena — has an important practical implication: harm often arises when internal states are projected outward. The angry internal state is real; the belief that the external person "caused" it and must change is a form of confusion between the internal and external. Developing clarity about what is *my* mental event and what is the external situation is a form of wisdom that reduces reactive harm.

**Doing More Good.** Understanding the relationship between *citta* and *cetasikā* — consciousness and its mental co-arisen factors — underpins the cultivation of wholesome mind-states. Every wholesome cetasika (faith, energy, mindfulness, concentration, wisdom, compassion, equanimity) co-arises with consciousness and shapes it. Cultivating any one of these factors lifts the quality of the entire mind-moment. This explains the importance of even small acts of virtue or attention: they condition the mental factors that arise in the next moment.

**Cultivating the Mind.** The pairs around *cittasamuṭṭhāna* (mind-originated) matter are relevant to understanding how mental states produce physical effects. Meditation produces mind-originated (*cittasamuṭṭhāna*) material phenomena — notably the lightness and ease of the body that accompanies deep concentration. Observing the arising and subsiding of these body-qualities is itself a meditation on *nāma-rūpa* (mind-and-matter) interdependence.

[[pi-dhammasangani-atthakatha#^1-553]]

#### Upādānagocchakaṃ

[[#^toc-1-2-11|↑↑↑]]

The Upādānagocchaka consists of six pairs built around *upādāna* — clinging. The commentary defines upādānā as states that *cling firmly and grasp with a strong grip* (*bhusaṃ ādiyanti daḷhaggāhaṃ gaṇhantīti upādānā*). The four clingings are: *kāmupādāna* (clinging to sensual pleasures), *diṭṭhupādāna* (clinging to views), *sīlabbatupādāna* (clinging to rules, rites, and rituals), and *attavādupādāna* (clinging to a doctrine of self). *Upādāniyā* dhammas are those that are objects of clinging — what the upādāna seizes upon and will not release.

**Doing Less Harm.** Clinging is deeper and more intractable than ordinary desire: it involves not merely wanting but tightly gripping. Much persistent harm in human life comes from clinging — particularly to views (*diṭṭhupādāna*) and to self-doctrine (*attavādupādāna*). Wars, family conflicts, and interpersonal cruelties are sustained by "I am right and you are wrong" (diṭṭhupādāna) and "my identity, my group, my worth" (attavādupādāna). Notice when an argument or conflict has the quality of *gripping* — this is the sign that upādāna is operative.

**Doing More Good.** Each of the four clingings is weakened by a specific practice: kāmupādāna by renunciation and contemplation of impermanence; diṭṭhupādāna by investigation and right view; sīlabbatupādāna by understanding that virtue is a *means* to liberation, not an end in itself; attavādupādāna by the sustained contemplation of non-self (*anattā*). Of these, the last is the most transformative — when the view of a stable, independent self is seen through by insight, all four clingings begin to loosen.

**Cultivating the Mind.** In meditation, clinging manifests as the resistance to letting go of a pleasant state, and as the desperate effort to maintain a particular meditative experience. This is *bhavupādāna* in the meditator's mind — clinging to meditative being. The practice instruction is the same as in daily life: observe the grip, note it as *upādāna*, and gently release. Non-clinging is not indifference — it is the natural state of the mind free from the compulsion to grasp.

#### Kilesagocchakaṃ

[[#^toc-1-2-12|↑↑↑]]

The Kilesagocchaka consists of eight pairs built around *kilesa* — defilement. The commentary notes that the meaning (*attha*) of the kilesa pairs is to be understood in the same way as the *saṃkiliṭṭha* triad — kilesā are states that *defile and torment the mind-stream* (*kiliñjanti parikiliñjantīti vā*). The ten defilements are: *lobha* (greed), *dosa* (hatred), *moha* (delusion), *māna* (conceit), *diṭṭhi* (wrong view), *vicikicchā* (doubt), *thīna* (sloth), *uddhacca* (restlessness), *ahirika* (lack of moral shame), and *anottappa* (lack of moral dread). *Saṃkilesikā* dhammas are those liable to defilement; *saṃkiliṭṭhā* are those already defiled.

**Doing Less Harm.** The last two kilesas — *ahirika* (lack of moral shame) and *anottappa* (lack of moral dread) — are particularly important for harm-prevention. The commentary elsewhere calls their opposites *hirī* and *ottappa* the "guardians of the world" (*lokapālā*): hirī refrains from wrongdoing out of inner self-respect; ottappa refrains out of awareness of consequences. When ahirika and anottappa are present — when a person has no inner shame and no fear of consequences — all other defilements can operate unchecked. Cultivating hirī and ottappa is therefore the foundation of not harming.

**Doing More Good.** The ten kilesas provide a comprehensive checklist for moral inventory. Beyond the gross defilements of greed, hatred, and delusion, the subtler kilesas require attention: *māna* (the conceit "I am better/equal/worse") operates even in meditative practitioners; *diṭṭhi* (wrong view) can persist even in disciplined practitioners who have not yet encountered right view; *vicikicchā* (doubt) can prevent taking the steps needed to progress. Regularly examine which of the ten kilesas are most active in your life and apply specific antidotes.

**Cultivating the Mind.** In meditation, the kilesas present themselves with surprising directness. Greed colors pleasant sensations with a tone of wanting. Hatred colors unpleasant sensations with aversion. Moha manifests as dullness and forgetfulness. Māna manifests in comparing one's meditation to others'. Vipassanā practice, particularly the contemplation of the arising and passing of phenomena, directly weakens the kilesas by exposing their conditioned, impersonal nature. See them not as "my defilements" but as conditioned events arising in the mind-stream — just as the Mātikā presents them: as *dhammā*, phenomena without an owner.

#### Piṭṭhidukaṃ

[[#^toc-1-2-13|↑↑↑]]

The Piṭṭhiduka consists of eighteen pairs placed at the *back* (*piṭṭha*) of the Abhidhamma matrix — hence the name. The commentary explains them: *kāmāvacarā* wander in the sense-sphere; *rūpāvacarā* in the form-sphere; *arūpāvacarā* in the formless-sphere. *Pariyāpannā* are included within the three-sphere round; *apariyāpannā* (Nibbāna and the supramundane path and fruit) are not. *Niyyānikā* cut the root of the round, take Nibbāna as their object, and lead out (*vaṭṭamūlaṃ chindantā nibbānaṃ ārammaṇaṃ katvā vaṭṭato niyyantīti*); *aniyyānikā* do not lead out. *Niyatā* are fixed — certain to produce their result at the next arising (*cutiyā anantaraṃ phaladāne niyatā*); *aniyatā* are unfixed. *Sauttarā* can be surpassed by something higher; *anuttarā* (Nibbāna) cannot. *Saraṇā* (with conflict) are those associated with the defilements that cause beings to cry out in anguish (*raṇanti etehīti raṇā*); *araṇā* are without such conflict.

**Doing Less Harm.** The pair *niyatā/aniyatā* carries a practical warning: some courses of action are fixed — they have a certain destination. This is most urgent with *micchattaniyata* actions: actions fixed toward the wrong destination (the five heinous acts, holding wrong views with certainty). Recognizing when one is near such a threshold is crucial. For most people most of the time, however, the situation is *aniyatā* — unfixed, still capable of being redirected. This is an opportunity, not a burden.

**Doing More Good.** The pair *niyyānikā/aniyyānikā* provides the crucial distinction between the noble path (which leads out of the round) and all other kusala states (which, however beneficial, remain within it). Prioritize the *niyyānikā* dhammas: the four paths and their corresponding insights. Every step of practice that brings these supramundane states closer — cultivation of the factors of awakening (*bojjhaṅgā*), the path factors (*maggaṅgā*), the perfections (*pāramitā*) — is oriented toward what is ultimately leading-out.

**Cultivating the Mind.** The pair *saraṇā/araṇā* — with conflict vs. without conflict — describes the difference between defilement-conditioned existence and the purity of the arahant. *Araṇā* dhammas are those free from the inner conflict (*raṇa*) of defilements — the constant friction of wanting and not-wanting, hoping and fearing. In meditation, there are moments when this inner conflict temporarily stills — moments of *araṇa-samāpatti* (attainment free of conflict). Cultivate these moments: they provide a taste of what liberation is like, and motivate continued practice.

#### Suttantikadukamātikā

[[#^toc-1-2-14|↑↑↑]]

The Suttantikadukamātikā consists of forty-two pairs drawn from the suttanta (*suttantikadukā*) tradition and placed at the end of the matrix. The commentary notes that these pairs, while not elaborated further in the Dhammasaṅgaṇī's main body, receive word-by-word explanation here in the Mātikā commentary itself, because their practical and pedagogical importance warrants it. These pairs range from epistemological distinctions (vijjābhāgino/avijjābhāgino — aligned with knowledge or ignorance), to ethical character (bālā/paṇḍitā — foolish or wise; tapanīyā/atapanīyā — tormenting or non-tormenting), to the core practical virtues, all the way to final liberation (vijjā/vimutti). They form the most practice-rich section of the Mātikā.

**Doing Less Harm.** Several pairs directly address the conditions for harm. *Ahirika/anottappa* — the absence of moral shame and moral dread — are the "tormenting" (*tapanīyā*) states par excellence: one who has neither shame nor moral dread regarding wrongdoing causes harm without restraint. The Mūlaṭīkā explains: *hirī* regards wrongdoing as loathsome (like dung); *ottappa* regards it as dangerous (like fire). Cultivating these two is the most direct prevention of harm, because they work *before* an action is committed. Equally, *dovacassatā* (unamenability to instruction) and *pāpamittatā* (bad friendship) are conditions for harm: an unteachable person surrounded by bad friends is at high risk of causing harm. Become amenable to correction (*sovacassa*) and seek good friendship (*kalyāṇamittatā*).

**Doing More Good.** The richest cluster of positive cultivation appears in the middle pairs of the Suttantikadukamātikā: *indriyesu guttadvāratā/bhojane mattaññutā* (guarding the sense doors, knowing measure in eating) are the foundational daily practices. *Sati/sampajaññañca* (mindfulness and clear comprehension) sustain all other practice throughout the day. *Samatho/vipassanā* name the two wings of meditation — and the commentary notes that the ideal is their *yuganaddha* (paired, balanced) development. *Sīlavisuddhi/diṭṭhivisuddhi* (purity of virtue and view) are not merely preparatory but are themselves high achievements. *Saṃvegañca saṃvejaniyesu ṭhānesu* (spiritual urgency regarding what is worthy of urgency) followed by *saṃviggassa yoniso padhānaṃ* (the wise effort of one who has been stirred): this sequence describes the transformation of existential dread into motivation for practice. Finally, the pair *asantuṭṭhitā kusalesu/appaṭivānitā padhānasmiṃ* — never being satisfied with wholesome states, never retreating from the effort — names the quality of the ideal practitioner.

**Cultivating the Mind.** The closing pairs of the Suttantikadukamātikā describe the culmination of the path. *Vijjā/vimutti* — true knowledge and liberation — are the final pair: *vijjā* is the direct knowing that destroys the āsavā; *vimutti* is the liberation that results. The Mūlaṭīkā clarifies: *vijjā* is knowing by fully illuminating (*vijānanato vijjā*); *vimutti* is freedom by full release (*vimuccanato vimutti*). *Khaye ñāṇa* — the knowledge of the exhaustion of the āsavā — is the knowledge of the noble path that destroys defilements; *anuppāde ñāṇa* — the knowledge of non-arising — is the knowledge of the noble fruit that confirms they will not arise again. These are the final fruits of the entire system described in the Mātikā. Hold them as the orientation-point of all practice.
