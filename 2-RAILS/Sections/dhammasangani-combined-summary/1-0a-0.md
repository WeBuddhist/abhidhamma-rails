## Tikamātikā: Combined Summary of Commentaries (1-0a-0)

This summary synthesizes the interpretations of the Atthakatha, Mūlaṭīkā, and Anuṭīkā on the *Dhammasaṅgaṇī*'s Tikamātikā, specifically focusing on the `1-0a-0` section which introduces the triads (starting with the *kusala* triad) and provides foundational definitions for key Abhidhamma terms.

### Atthakatha

The Atthakatha begins by outlining the structure of the *mātikā* (matrix), noting it consists of 22 triads (*tikā*) and 100 dyads (*dukā*), which are further classified as either exhaustive (*nippadesa*) or non-exhaustive (*sappadesa*). It then provides crucial definitions for the terms in the first triad:
*   **Kusala**: The word is used in four senses: health/freedom from illness (`ārogya`), faultlessness (`anavajja`), skill (`cheka`), and happy result (`sukhavipāka`). Etymologically, it refers to things that destroy or shake off contemptible things, or things to be grasped by knowledge (`kusa`). Its characteristic is being faultless and having a happy result.
*   **Dhamma**: While the term can mean scripture, root, quality, or soullessness/lifelessness, here it specifically denotes soullessness/absence of a living entity (`nissattanijjīvatā`).
*   **Akusala**: Defined as the contrary opposite of *kusala*, not merely its absence. Its characteristic is being blameworthy and having a painful result.
*   **Abyākata**: These are phenomena "undeclared" as either *kusala* or *akusala*, characterized by having no resultant nature (i.e., they do not produce karmic results).

### Mūlaṭīkā

The Mūlaṭīkā refines and defends the Atthakatha's definitions against potential logical objections:
*   **Dhamma**: It clarifies that *dhammas* are phenomena that "bear their own nature" (`sabhāvaṃ dhārentīti`), not entities grasped by consciousness like a "being" or "soul".
*   **Akusala as Opposite**: It provides a logical proof that *akusala* is a contrary opposite (`tappaṭipakkha`) and not just the absence of *kusala* (`kusalābhāvamatta`): if it were merely the absence of *kusala*, the third category of *abyākata* (undeclared) would be redundant. The existence of the third category proves *akusala* is a specific, active opposition.
*   **Characteristics**: It unpacks the characteristic of *kusala* (`anavajjasukhavipākalakkhaṇā`), explaining that "faultless" (`anavajja`) points to the purity of its active nature (present state), while "happy result" (`sukhavipāka`) points to the purity of its outcome (future state).

### Anuṭīkā

The Anuṭīkā delves deeper into the grammatical and philosophical nuances of the terms:
*   **The Prefix 'A-'**: It offers an extensive analysis of the negative prefix `a-` (or `na-`), noting it can denote many things (negation of association, condition, nature, etc.). In the case of *akusala*, it confirms it strictly means a contrary opposite.
*   **Meaning of 'Sukha'**: It provides a critical clarification regarding the term `sukha` in `sukhavipāka` (happy result). It states that `sukha` here must be understood as a synonym for "desirable" (`iṭṭhapariyāyavacanaṃ`), because literal pleasant feeling cannot be the result of all *kusala* phenomena (e.g., material forms produced by karma are desirable results, but are not themselves "feelings" of pleasure).
*   **Dhamma Categorization**: It further categorizes *dhammas* based on how they bear their nature, distinguishing between conditioned (*saṅkhata*), unconditioned (*asaṅkhata*), and conceptual (*paññatti*) phenomena.

### Synthesis

The three commentaries demonstrate a clear progression of scholastic refinement. The Atthakatha establishes the baseline semantic and etymological definitions of the fundamental Abhidhamma categories (*kusala*, *akusala*, *abyākata*, *dhamma*). The Mūlaṭīkā steps in to logically fortify these definitions, ensuring that the tripartite division is philosophically sound (especially proving *akusala* is an active opposite, not a passive absence). Finally, the Anuṭīkā provides the most granular level of precision, using grammatical analysis to lock down the exact meaning of prefixes and clarifying that terms like *sukha* in compound definitions must be interpreted broadly (as "desirable") to maintain systemic consistency across all types of phenomena.
