# Day 7 — The First Wholesome Mind

## 1. Today's Chanting Guide

We enter the *Cittuppādakaṇḍaṃ* — the Chapter on the Arising of Consciousness — the opening and heart of the Dhammasaṅgaṇī. Where the Mātikā listed categories of phenomena as bare terms, this chapter asks the deeper question: *what actually happens inside a mind when something wholesome arises?*

The passage opens with a question that structures everything that follows: *Katame dhammā kusalā?* — "Which phenomena are wholesome?" The answer comes as a single long breath naming 56 mental factors (*cetasikā*), all co-arising in the very first moment of wholesome sense-sphere consciousness — a mind accompanied by joy, associated with knowledge. Verses 2 through 26 then define each factor individually, beginning with contact and moving through feeling, perception, intention, mind, applied thought, sustained thought, rapture, pleasure, one-pointedness, the five spiritual faculties, four path factors, and the powers of faith and energy.

This is the Abhidhamma's way of saying: a wholesome moment is not a vague mood. It is precise. Every quality it contains can be named, defined, and recognized.

Today's question to carry into the chant: *Right now, as you prepare to chant — what is present in this mind?*

## 4. Reading for Meaning

### Which Phenomena are Wholesome?

At the time when the sense-sphere wholesome mind has arisen — accompanied by joy, associated with knowledge, taking a form, sound, smell, taste, touch, or mental object as its object, or whatever it may be — at that time there is **contact**, there is **feeling**, there is **perception**, there is **intention**, there is **mind**, there is **applied thought**, there is **sustained thought**, there is **rapture**, there is **pleasure**, there is **one-pointedness of mind**; there is the faculty of faith, the faculty of energy, the faculty of mindfulness, the faculty of concentration, the faculty of wisdom, the faculty of mind, the faculty of joy, the faculty of life; there is right view, right thought, right effort, right mindfulness, right concentration; there is the power of faith, the power of energy, the power of mindfulness, the power of concentration, the power of wisdom, the power of moral shame, the power of moral dread; there is non-greed, non-hatred, non-delusion, non-covetousness, non-ill-will, right view; there is moral shame, moral dread; there is tranquillity of the mental body, tranquillity of mind; lightness of the mental body, lightness of mind; pliancy of the mental body, pliancy of mind; wieldiness of the mental body, wieldiness of mind; proficiency of the mental body, proficiency of mind; uprightness of the mental body, uprightness of mind; there is mindfulness, clear comprehension, serenity, insight, effort, non-distraction — or whatever other co-arisen immaterial phenomena there are at that time. **These phenomena are wholesome.** *(v. 1)*

## 5. Pāli Word of the Day

### PHASSA — Contact

**Pronunciation:** PHAS-sa — the "ph" is an aspirated "p," with a breath of air behind it; the double "ss" is held a beat longer than a single "s."

**Literal sense:** From the root √*phas*, "to touch." The Dhammasaṅgaṇī defines it: *phasso phusanā saṃphusanā saṃphusitattaṃ* — "contact, the touching, the state of being touched." Three synonyms for the same event, each capturing a slightly different angle: the act, the quality of that act, the resulting state.

**In today's passage:** Phassa is the first of the 56 factors named in verse 1, and the first to be individually defined in verse 2. It is the meeting-point of three elements: the sense-base (e.g., the eye-base, or here the mind-base), the object (a form, sound, smell, taste, touch, or mental object), and consciousness. Without contact, none of the other 55 factors can arise. It is the hinge on which a mind-moment turns.

**Why it matters:** Every experience — pleasant or painful, wholesome or unwholesome — begins at the moment of contact. To see phassa arising is to watch the very start of a mind-moment. The Abhidhamma's precision here is not abstract philosophy; it is a map of what is happening right now, in this session of practice.
