# Day 8 — The Two Bright Dhammas

## 1. Today's Chanting Guide

Yesterday the first wholesome mind arose and we named 56 of its qualities, defining each through verse 26. Today we complete the Padabhājanī — the Word-by-Word Analysis — with verses 27 through 57, bringing the full account of the first *kāmāvacara kusala* citta to a close.

Among today's definitions, two stand out as a pair: *hirī* (moral shame) and *ottappa* (moral dread). These are called the two bright dhammas (*dve sukkhadhammā*) that guard the world. Hirī is the inward sense of shame at wrongdoing — a conscience oriented toward one's own dignity. Ottappa is the outward dread of wrongdoing — concern for the harm it brings to others and one's reputation. Together they form the moral immune system of the mind. Where these are absent, the door to all unwholesome states stands open.

The closing verse today seals the entire analysis: "Padabhājanīyaṃ. Paṭhamabhāṇavāro" — the Word Analysis is complete. The first chanting portion is complete. All 56 factors of the first wholesome mind have been defined.

Today's question to carry into the chant: *Can I locate anything in this moment that resembles hirī — a natural reluctance to act against my own integrity?*

## 5. Pāli Word of the Day

### HIRĪ — Moral Shame

**Pronunciation:** HI-rī — the "h" is breathy; the final "ī" is held long, like "ee."

**Literal sense:** The Dhammasaṅgaṇī defines hirī as: *hirīyati hiriyitabbena hirīyati pāpakānaṃ akusalānaṃ dhammānaṃ samāpattiyā* — "it is ashamed of what ought to be ashamed of; it is ashamed of the attainment of bad, unwholesome phenomena." The word points inward: hirī is a sense of moral self-respect, a natural reluctance to violate one's own integrity.

**Its pair, ottappa:** Hirī and ottappa always appear together in the Dhammasaṅgaṇī. Where hirī looks inward (shame in relation to oneself), *ottappa* looks outward (dread of the consequences and harm to others). Together they are called the two *sukkhadhammā* — the two bright or luminous dhammas. The commentary tradition describes them as guardians of the world, because wherever they are present, harmful action is restrained.

**In the practice:** The *pi-1-practice.md* rail notes that hirī and ottappa are listed among the 56 factors of every kāmāvacara kusala citta. They are not optional extras but structural features of any wholesome mind in the sense-sphere. Cultivating them means cultivating sensitivity to the quality of one's own intentions — not as guilt, but as natural moral discernment.
