# Audience Profile — Daily Tipitaka

Sits alongside `requirements.md` and `termbase.md`: requirements say *how* the daily sessions are shaped; termbase says *which words*; this file says *who they are for*. The session-generation skills load all three before producing a day.

This profile covers the Daily Tipitaka plan as a whole — the "200-day road to the ITCC" journey that prepares lay practitioners to take part in the International Tipitaka Chanting Ceremony at Bodhgayā each December. This year (2026) the ITCC chants the Abhidhamma Piṭaka; future years will chant other sections of the Tipiṭaka. The plan's audience is defined by the ITCC, not by the section in focus that year. Per-language nuance (Bengali vs. Hindi vs. English readers) lives in each Translation track's own `audience.md` under `3-TRANSFORMATIONS/Translations/` — see `delegates_to` in the frontmatter.

## 1. Demographics and Region

- **Age range:** 18–70, with the middle weighted toward 30–55. Sub-population of older monastics (60+); sub-population of college-age practitioners (18–25) joining family pilgrimage groups.
- **Geography (primary and secondary regions):**
  - *Primary:* Bengali-speaking regions of South Asia — West Bengal (especially greater Kolkata) and Bangladesh (especially Chittagong-area Barua communities). Maharashtra (especially Nagpur and the wider Vidarbha region — Ambedkarite Buddhist heartland). Uttar Pradesh, centred on the Sankisa pilgrimage area and surrounding Hindi-speaking belt.
  - *Secondary:* The broader Theravāda world represented at the ITCC last year — practitioners from roughly 20 countries including Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Thailand, Nepal, Bhutan, Vietnam, Indonesia, and the South Asian diaspora in the UK, US, Canada, Australia, and the Gulf.
- **Language background:** Bengali as L1 for the largest cluster; Marathi (frequently with Hindi as L2) for the Maharashtra cluster; Hindi as L1 for the UP cluster; assorted L1s across the international cohort. English literacy varies widely — strong in urban professionals and the diaspora, weaker in rural and Ambedkarite-community readers. Pāli is recognised as a sacred chanting language by most but read by very few.
- **Monastic / lay / mixed:** Mixed, with **lay strongly predominant** — the 25,000 last year were overwhelmingly lay pilgrims. A monastic minority is highly experienced; the plan must serve both but is calibrated for the lay majority.
- **Gender mix:** Roughly even across the lay majority. Some regional skews (e.g. women's chanting groups in Bengal; mixed-gender Ambedkarite study circles in Maharashtra). No content should assume a male reader.
- **Other:** A significant share of the Maharashtra and UP audience comes through the Navayana / Ambedkarite Buddhist tradition — strong Buddhist identity, deep relationship to Dr. Ambedkar's writings, but limited prior exposure to canonical Pāli material. Treat this as a feature: the plan is often their *first* sustained encounter with the Tipiṭaka itself.

## 2. Prior Knowledge and Reading Level

- **Buddhist background:** Strong Buddhist identity, limited canonical exposure. Most readers know the Buddha's life story, the Four Noble Truths, the basics of refuge and precepts; few have read sustained passages from the Tipiṭaka in any language, and almost none have studied the Abhidhamma before.
- **Familiarity with Pāli:** Recognises Pāli as the sacred chanting language; recognises the cadence of the *Namo tassa* / refuges / *paritta* chants from group recitation. Cannot read or parse Pāli words independently. The chanting tradition is **aural** for most of this audience, not textual.
- **Familiarity with the Abhidhamma specifically:** **First encounter** for nearly everyone. A few may have heard the names of the seven books; almost none have studied a primer. The plan should not assume the reader can place the Dhammasaṅgaṇī in the seven-book sequence on day one.
- **General education level / target reading grade:** Wide spread. The plan must work at roughly an **8th-grade reading level** in each target language — the same calibration the NIV uses for the Bible in English — so that a schoolteacher, a shopkeeper, and a college student can all follow the same day without it patronising the first or losing the second.
- **Comfortable with footnotes, diacritics, transliteration?** Diacritics on Pāli words: yes, tolerable on first use with a pronunciation hint, but **never piled up**. Footnotes: minimise — most reading happens on a phone where footnotes are friction. Transliteration: standard IAST for Pāli throughout; Devanāgarī or Bengali script renderings may be added per-language stream where the local audience reads better that way.

## 3. Use Cases and Reading Settings

- **Private silent reading:** The primary daily mode. ~5–15 minutes, usually morning, often on a phone before the day begins.
- **Read-aloud or group reading:** Common in temple settings in Kolkata, in Ambedkarite study circles in Nagpur, in Sankisa-area pilgrimage groups, and in monastic communities internationally. The seven-step structure is designed to work read-aloud — sentences must pass the read-aloud test (no clauses that stall the tongue).
- **Chanting / liturgical use:** Central. Step 6 (Chanting in Pāli) is the practice the entire plan trains toward — the ability to chant alongside the Bodhgayā gathering in December.
- **Mobile app vs. print vs. desktop vs. PDF:** **Phone-first.** Print is secondary (some local sanghas may print weekly digests). Desktop is rare; PDF is occasional. Every layout decision assumes a phone screen and intermittent connectivity.
- **Session length:** 5–15 minutes daily; longer (20–30) on the weekend or when the practitioner is on retreat.
- **Frequency:** Daily for 200 days, anchored to the ITCC date. Practitioners *will* miss days — the per-arc structure (`plans/plan-NNN-…`) exists so the next arc can be entered cleanly without catching up on every missed day.
- **Accompanying materials:** Often a teacher or sangha leader; sometimes audio recitation; sometimes alone with a phone. The plan should work in all three.

## 4. Motivations and Pain Points

- **What draws them to the Tipiṭaka:** A real pilgrimage. They are going (or want to go) to Bodhgayā in December, and want to arrive prepared rather than as a spectator. For many, the ITCC is a once-in-a-lifetime gathering at the site of the Buddha's enlightenment, and they want the chanting to feel like *theirs* — not like sound they witness from outside.
- **What they hope to get out of this transformation:** Three concrete things, in roughly this order — (1) confidence to chant a passage aloud in Pāli without feeling lost; (2) some genuine sense of what the day's passage *means*, not just how it sounds; (3) a daily rhythm that makes the road to Bodhgayā feel like a path they are walking, not a date on a calendar.
- **What frustrates them about existing translations or study materials:** Existing Abhidhamma material is dense, academic, often Victorian English (Rhys Davids) or technical Pāli–Sanskrit hybrid prose. It assumes the reader has studied the seven books, can hold Pāli compounds in their head, and is comfortable with footnote-dense pages. Most existing material is also pitched at Buddhist Studies academics or at Theravāda monastics with prior commentarial training — neither of which matches this audience. Materials that come from a Western academic register can also feel culturally unfamiliar to a Maharashtra or Sankisa reader whose Buddhist context is devotional and community-based.
- **What would make them stop reading:** Pāli-term pileups (three new technical terms in one sentence); footnote density on a phone screen; assumption of prior Abhidhamma knowledge on day one; sessions that consistently run past 15 minutes; an academic tone that feels like a textbook rather than a daily practice; sectarian or polemical framing that excludes Ambedkarite, Mahāyāna-adjacent, or non-Theravāda readers.
- **Stretch goal — what would delight them:** Walking into the chanting hall at Bodhgayā in December and recognising the passage being chanted, knowing what it means, and being able to join the recitation without losing their place. A felt sense, by day 100, that the Abhidhamma is no longer a foreign mountain but a landscape they have been crossing day by day. Quiet, accumulating confidence rather than dramatic transformation.

## 5. Non-audience

The plan is **not** built for the following readers. Decisions that compromise the calibration above for these readers should be reversed:

- **Buddhist Studies academics and PhD-level researchers** — they should work from `2-RAILS/` directly, or from one of the Translation tracks. The Daily Tipitaka deliberately sacrifices technical precision for daily-practice accessibility.
- **Readers who want a self-help, mindfulness, or stress-reduction product** — this is devotional pilgrimage preparation anchored to a specific ceremony, not a wellbeing app.
- **Readers who have not yet decided whether Buddhism is for them** — the plan assumes the reader is already inside the Buddhist tradition (lay or monastic, any school) and is committed to the ITCC chanting context. A "Buddhism 101" track is the wrong shape; if there is demand for one, it belongs in `Adaptations/`, not here.
- **Readers wanting a Mahāyāna or Vajrayāna devotional reading** — the Pāli chanting tradition the plan trains for is Theravāda. Tibetan and Chinese language streams exist to *serve readers in those languages* on the road to ITCC, not to translate the plan into a Mahāyāna or Vajrayāna register.

## 6. Persona vignettes

These three composite readers cover the bulk of the lay majority. The session-generation skill should be able to imagine each of them encountering a draft day and ask whether it works for them.

### Anjali — Kolkata, West Bengal

Anjali is 34, a primary-school teacher in a Kolkata suburb. She grew up in a Bengali Buddhist (Barua) family; her grandmother taught her the refuges and the *Buddhaṃ saraṇaṃ gacchāmi* chant when she was small, but no one in the family has studied any canonical text. She reads Bengali fluently and English competently; her Pāli is a few memorised lines from temple. She has two children, and her practice time is the 10 minutes after the school-run drop-off, on her phone, before she opens her teaching prep. She is going to Bodhgayā for the first time in December with her local sangha and hopes by then she will be able to recognise at least *some* of what the gathering chants — right now, when she hears Pāli, it sounds beautiful but opaque. The plan works for Anjali when the day's English or Bengali reading lets her hold the *meaning* of the passage in mind first, so that when she gets to the Pāli step she is following a chant whose shape she already knows.

### Ramesh — Nagpur, Maharashtra

Ramesh is 47, a small-shop owner in Nagpur. His family converted to Buddhism in his grandfather's generation, following Dr. Ambedkar; he has read Ambedkar carefully and identifies strongly as a Buddhist, but the canonical Tipiṭaka has always been adjacent to his practice rather than central to it. He reads Marathi as his first language and Hindi comfortably; his English is limited to functional shop work. His phone is his only reading device. He is going to Bodhgayā for the first time, mostly because his local Buddhist samiti is going as a group, and he is quietly curious about what the *Abhidhamma* actually is — he has heard the word but never read a line of it. The plan works for Ramesh when the Hindi stream (since Marathi isn't yet published — see open question below) reads like a conversation his samiti could have, not a textbook; when the Pāli chant is introduced gently, with the meaning made plain first; and when the underlying message stays compatible with the Buddhism he already practices.

### Sunita — Sankisa pilgrimage area, Uttar Pradesh

Sunita is 22, a third-year college student in a small town near Sankisa. Her family has been visiting Sankisa for as long as she can remember — the site where the Buddha is said to have descended after teaching the Abhidhamma to his mother in Tāvatiṃsa. She reads Hindi as her first language and is improving her English at university. Her Buddhist exposure is more *cultural* than *textual*: she knows the pilgrimage circuit, the festival days, the major stories, but has never sat down with a Buddhist book. She is the most likely of the three to be reading the plan on the move — phone, train, between classes — and the most likely to share screenshots with friends if a particular day's reading lands well. The plan works for Sunita when each day is *short enough to finish in one sitting between classes*, when it makes the connection between this year's passages and the place she has been visiting all her life, and when the Pāli step gives her something memorable enough to mention to her grandmother that evening.

## Open questions for the next pass

- **Marathi stream.** A large fraction of the primary audience reads Marathi as L1. The current plan publishes Hindi but not Marathi; for now, Maharashtra readers route through the Hindi stream. Adding a Marathi stream (`days/mr/`, with a corresponding Translation track) is the highest-impact extension of the plan's reach and should be considered before the next ITCC.
- **Bengali stream parity.** The plan currently publishes `pi/en/bo/zh/hi/` for days; given that the largest single audience is Bengali-speaking, a `bn/` stream that consumes the existing `bn-Contemporary-Bengali-Abhidhamma` Translation track is also high-priority.
- **Aural vs. textual emphasis.** The audience's relationship to Pāli is overwhelmingly aural. Whether the plan needs an audio companion (chanting recordings keyed to each day's Step 6) is a product question the brief should resolve.
